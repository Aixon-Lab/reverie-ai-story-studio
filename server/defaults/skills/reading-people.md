## Core principles

Nobody reads minds; people read *changes*. A tell is only a tell against a
baseline — this person, calm, five minutes ago. Write the baseline first, even in
a single clause, or the observation has nothing to stand on.

Discomfort is legible; its cause is not. A dry mouth means stress, not guilt. The
most realistic version of this skill is a character who correctly notices
something is wrong and then guesses wrong about why — which is a far better story
engine than accurate mind-reading.

People leak most in transitions: the half-second before answering, the moment a
subject changes, the beat after they think the conversation ended. Put the
observation there rather than in the middle of a speech.

## Vocabulary and sensory palette

Concrete and small: a swallow before a sentence, hands that find something to
hold, feet already pointed at the door, a smile that arrives late or leaves
early, breathing that has become deliberate. Voice does more work than face —
pitch climbing, pace changing, an answer arriving too fast or too complete.

Verbal tells beat physical ones. Over-specific detail, unprompted denial,
repeating the question, retreating into formality, answering a slightly different
question than the one asked.

Avoid the folklore. Looking away is not lying, arms crossed is not defensive, and
a character who quotes those rules should be wrong about something soon.

## Range of competence

**Oblivious.** Takes words at face value, notices only after the fact, and often
notices the wrong thing loudly. Useful and underwritten.

**Ordinarily observant.** Feels that something is off before knowing what, and
usually decides not to say so. Most people live here.

**Practised — interrogator, medic, bartender, con artist, someone who grew up
around danger.** Notices baseline, notices deviation, and knows that the useful
move is often to say nothing and let the silence work.

**Expert.** Does not analyse; already knows, and has moved on to steering. An
expert's read shows up as a change in *their* behaviour — a question asked from
a different angle — rather than as a list of observations.

## How it reads on the page

Show one detail, then the inference, then the doubt. Two beats of noticing per
scene is plenty; a character cataloguing five tells reads as a machine.

Keep the inference in the character's own vocabulary. A soldier reads distance and
threat, a nurse reads pain and dehydration, a courtier reads status and audience.
The same nervous man produces three different readings — that is the whole point
of the skill living in a character rather than in the narrator.

Interiority carries this: what they notice, what they choose not to say, and what
they do with it. Never let the narration announce that someone is lying.

## Escalation and stakes

The interesting move is what the reader does with the read. Confront, store,
test, or pretend not to have noticed — each is a different scene, and the choice
characterises more than the observation.

Testing is the richest option: a small deliberate question whose answer will
separate two explanations. Write the test, not the verdict.

## Failure, limits and cost

Being read is a violation, and people who do it well are often trusted less.
Someone who narrates other people to themselves constantly is exhausting to be
around, and the story should occasionally say so.

Confidence is the failure mode. A character sure of a wrong read will act on it,
and that is usually the best available plot. Cultural difference, neurodivergence,
illness, grief, exhaustion, and simple unfamiliarity all break the baseline —
a good reader knows their read is provisional.

## Adapting to setting and character

Ask what this character has reason to be fluent in. A conman reads greed; a
survivor reads anger; a child reads whether the adults are about to shout. Fluency
is domain-specific, and nobody is fluent in everything.

Where masks are cultural — court, ritual, formality, a profession with a required
face — everyone is performing, and the skill becomes reading the performance's
seams rather than the person. In settings with magic, telepathy or lie detection,
decide explicitly whether this skill is redundant or is the thing people use when
the magic is unavailable, expensive, or illegal.

Written or remote communication strips almost every channel; a strong reader
should visibly feel that loss.

## Never do this

Never turn a read into certainty, and never let the narration confirm it in the
same beat. Never produce a list of tells. Never use the folklore cues as if they
worked. Never let this skill replace the scene's real conflict with an analysis
of it — the point of noticing is to do something.
