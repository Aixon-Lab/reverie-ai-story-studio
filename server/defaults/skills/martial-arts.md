## Core principles

Violence is decided before it looks decided. Distance, balance and initiative are
the three things a fight is actually about; strikes are only how those advantages
get spent. Write the fight as a conversation about space — who is closing it, who
is stealing it back — and the choreography takes care of itself.

Weight moves before the limb does. A committed strike starts at the floor and
arrives late; that lag is what a trained opponent reads and an untrained one
misses. Every exchange should show one person acting and the other answering a
beat behind, until someone stops answering.

Nobody fights at full capacity for long. Ninety seconds of real effort empties
most people. Fear makes hands clumsy and hearing narrow. If a fight runs long,
the prose should get shorter and worse-lit, not more elaborate.

## Vocabulary and sensory palette

Contact has texture: the flat slap of a forearm block, the deadened thud of a
body shot that lands on breath rather than bone, the specific wrongness of a
joint moving where joints do not. Prefer verbs with a weight class — *shove*,
*clip*, *drive*, *smother*, *fold* — over generic *hit* and *attack*.

What a body reports: taste of copper, the ear that stops working after a shot to
the head, hands going hot then numb, the floor arriving faster than expected.
Adrenaline edits perception — colour drains, time stutters, a small irrelevant
detail becomes enormous.

Sound matters more than sight in close quarters. Breath, scuffle, cloth, the
noise a person makes involuntarily when winded — never a clean cinematic *whoosh*.

## Range of competence

**Never trained.** Wide swinging blows from the shoulder, eyes shut at the moment
of contact, grabbing rather than striking, gassing out in seconds, and afterwards
shaking so hard they cannot hold anything. Untrained people mostly wrestle. They
also freeze, which is the most commonly omitted and most realistic beat.

**Rough experience — brawler, soldier, someone who grew up fighting.** Economical
and ugly. Headbutts, elbows, the environment used as a weapon, hitting first
because waiting has cost them before. Fluent at starting fights, poor at
finishing them cleanly.

**Trained.** Stance that survives movement, guard that returns, strikes thrown in
combinations because a single strike is a gift. Reads the shoulder, not the fist.
Ends things in exchanges rather than blows.

**Master.** Does less, not more. Positions so the opponent's best option is still
bad. Often looks unimpressive to a spectator — the fight ends early and nobody
sees why. A master fighting an amateur is not a fight; it is a decision.

## How it reads on the page

Alternate the camera between intention and consequence: one clause on what is
attempted, one on what it costs. Long paragraphs kill fight tension — cut lines
short as the exchange tightens, and let a single sentence carry the moment
something breaks.

Three exchanges is usually the whole fight. Beyond that, escalate the stakes or
end it; a fourth round of trading blows reads as choreography, not danger.

Give each fighter one habit and keep it: the one who circles left, the one who
talks, the one who never stops moving forward. Recognition is what makes a fight
feel like these two people rather than two bodies.

## Escalation and stakes

Fights escalate along one of three axes — weapons, numbers, or intent. Moving
along any of them should be a visible choice by a character, not a drift in the
prose. The moment someone reaches for a weapon is a story beat, not a stunt.

The most useful question in a fight scene is what each person is trying to
achieve: to leave, to win, to hurt, to be seen fighting, to buy thirty seconds.
Almost nobody is trying to kill, and when someone is, the register should change
completely.

## Failure, limits and cost

Techniques fail. Locks slip, throws land badly, a perfect strike hits an elbow.
A skilled character who never misses is less impressive than one who misses and
recovers.

Injury is not a status bar. A hand that broke in the second exchange stays broken
for the rest of the story, and it changes how doors get opened and cups get held.
Aftermath — shaking, nausea, the delayed cry, the fact that neither person can
speak normally for a while — is where a fight becomes memorable.

## Adapting to setting and character

Ask three questions before writing a single blow: what does this character know,
what does this world allow, and what are they wearing. A duelling culture, a
back-alley with no rules, and a bar fight where everyone is a colleague on Monday
produce three different fights from the same two bodies.

If firearms exist and are present, hand-to-hand is what happens when something
has already gone wrong; write it as desperation, not choice. In a low-tech era,
reach and armour dominate, and unarmoured people avoid contact far more than
fiction suggests. Where fighting is ritual or sport, rules and audience shape
everything — including what a loss actually costs.

Environment is a participant: footing, light, walls, furniture, the number of
exits. A character who uses the room is characterised by it.

## Never do this

Never let a character fight above what they plausibly know — a frightened student
swinging a chair is a fight, and it is not martial arts. Never narrate a move by
its technical name unless the character would think of it that way. Never let a
fight run on pure choreography with no intention behind it. Never leave the
aftermath out. And never reach for this at all when the scene is really about
something else and the violence is only decoration.
